Which returned parts need a closer look, and which test should run first?

A returned part arrives with a complaint text. The clue that would justify a targeted load test is usually buried in it — and there are too many reports to read closely.

The rule this page is built on

AI suggests.Code checks.A technician decides.

No card can close, scrap or return a part. Those states do not exist in the data model.

  1. 01

    It reads. Each complaint is read for the component group, safety flag, symptoms, conditions, fault codes and mileage, and every clue carries the exact words it came from; the code checks that each quote appears verbatim and drops the ones that do not.

  2. 02

    It routes. Safety-relevant cases go first, similar past cases are found, the next test is suggested with its reasons and the information still missing, and weekly complaint counts are watched for unusual spikes.

  3. 03

    It does not decide. It never closes, scraps or returns a part — a technician accepts or changes every case, with a reason.

01 The flow

Seven steps, and only one of them is a model.

The model reads the text. Code checks the quotes, applies the hard rules and runs the rulebook. A person makes the decision. That split is the point: the model is useful exactly where language is messy, and it is never trusted with the outcome.

03 Results

Two models, free baselines, one evaluation set.

Every score below comes from the replay of the committed run, with 95% bootstrap intervals. Macro-F1 is the primary metric and counts an “unclear” answer as wrong. The baselines cost nothing and set the floor the models have to beat.

Component group

macro-F1 · higher is better

The TF-IDF baseline is a bag-of-words classifier on the past-case library. The models have to beat it by a wide margin to justify their cost.

Safety decision

recall and precision · higher is better

Recall is the share of coded crash, fire or injury cases the hard rules caught. Precision is the share of flagged cases that were coded. High recall with modest precision means many extra cases to read, not missed safety.

04 Trend watch

A spike is a question, not a recall.

Complaint counts are cheap and need no model. The rule flags a week with at least three complaints that also clears the Poisson 99.9% upper limit of its own trailing 26-week mean. The useful test is not how many alerts fire, but whether they beat random weeks of the same series.

05 Limits

What this page cannot tell you.

Four limits matter more than any single score. They are part of the result, not a disclaimer bolted on at the end.

01

US vehicle complaints stand in for return reports

A vehicle owner's complaint is not a lab return report. It is the same kind of free text, which is why it can stand in for the reading task — but the failure context, the test history and the customer's wording are different.

02

The coded labels are noisy

Crash, fire, injury and component fields come from a public coding process, not from an engineer's finding. Safety and group scores inherit that noise, and no amount of model quality removes it.

03

The rulebook is an example

Seven condition and symptom kinds map to suggested tests. A lab would replace them with its own test catalogue, inspection points and escalation rules; the hard rules around safety stay as they are.

04

Condition agreement is not accuracy

There is no ground truth for “moisture” or “vibration”. The condition number is agreement with a fixed keyword rule, and it is labelled that way everywhere it appears.
